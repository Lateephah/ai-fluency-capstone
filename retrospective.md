# Retrospective  for the person I was in Week 1

Dear Week 1 me,

When you hear "portfolio," you're going to think it means showing everything, every model you've trained, every domain you've touched, NLP and computer vision and whatever the internship throws at you next. You're going to draft a proof statement that tries to hold all of it at once, and it's going to feel like the honest, credible thing to do. It isn't wrong, exactly. It's just not what a portfolio is for.

Here's what actually happens: you get told to cut it down to one claim, one project, one person. It feels like limiting yourself. You'll worry, specifically, about when you ever get to show the rest of what you can do if you're only allowed one door for a stranger to walk through. I want to tell you that worry goes away, but it doesn't, not fully. I'm writing this still holding some of it. What changes isn't the worry disappearing. It's understanding why the trade is worth making anyway: a portfolio that tries to say everything reads as saying nothing in particular. What actually convinces someone isn't range, it's the moment they read one line and think "this person already knows exactly what I need." That's a product-like feature, not a resume summary. You can't get that feeling from a list.

The claim itself moved more than I expected, too. My first real draft spanned NLP, computer vision, and the internship's search-ranking work, three domains in one sentence. It took a pressure-test conversation to actually see that the sentence could describe almost any competent junior ML person, not specifically me. It got cut down to one thing: LexGuard, one audience, one action. Even the audience needed narrowing twice, once from "SME owners" to a legal-tech founder specifically. And later, when I reviewed a friend's portfolio, I caught the exact same vagueness in her claim before I'd have caught it as clearly in my own which told me the lesson had actually landed, not just been agreed with in the moment.

The bigger shift is in how I work with AI now, not just what I believe about portfolios. There's a framework from this internship, the 4 Ds and the one that actually stuck is discernment: staying in the loop as a cyborg, not a passenger. Concretely, that meant catching a real contradiction in my own CV instead of letting a draft paper over it. It meant pushing back when Claude misread a job posting's requirements, and making it re-quote the exact source text before either of us trusted an answer. It meant tracing a recall statistic back through my own evaluation notebook instead of accepting a clean-sounding summary of it. None of that is exotic. It's just refusing the smooth, agreeable answer when it hasn't actually been checked.

Three things I'd tell you are genuinely transferable, not just true for this one project: a narrow, verifiable claim earns trust faster than an honest, broad one, even when narrow feels like underselling yourself. Discernment has to be a habit you practice, not a value you hold, it shows up as actually re-checking sources, not as a general commitment to being careful. And real diligence means finding your own failures before someone else does, which is a different skill than building the thing in the first place.

What I'd build next, I genuinely don't have a clean answer for that yet, and I'd rather tell you that honestly than invent a tidy plan. I have a second case study already drafted, Content Opportunity Scoring and I've realized it doesn't actually belong next to LexGuard, because it's a different domain answering a different audience's question. Adding it would recreate the exact vagueness I just spent weeks cutting. The real next step isn't "add a case." It's going back through everything I've built, grouping it by similarity, deciding on one niche to actually commit to, and only then letting the claim get bolder grounded by depth in one direction, not breadth across all of them.

So Latifah, several weeks and one narrowed claim later
